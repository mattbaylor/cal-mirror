Calendar Mirror

Privacy

Last updated: September 2026 — DRAFT for 2.0

Your calendar belongs to you — not to me, and not to a server. Calendar Mirror has no account and makes no network requests of its own. It never sends your calendar anywhere. The one optional feature that does use a server — the request page — is built so the server cannot see your calendar either, and this page says exactly what it holds instead.

What the app does

You tell Calendar Mirror which calendar to copy and where to copy it. It reads events from the source and writes copies into the destination, using Apple's Calendar framework on your device. That's the whole job. The list of pairs you set up is stored only on your device.

No collection, no tracking

No analytics. No advertising or tracking SDKs. No behavioral data. No crash or usage telemetry sent to me. Calendar Mirror ships with none of that and never will — surveillance isn't a feature I'm interested in building.

No credentials, ever

Calendar Mirror works through calendars that are already set up on your device, so it never asks for, sees, or stores any account passwords or tokens.

You choose how much crosses over

Because of that, Calendar Mirror lets each pair decide what a copy actually contains. A copy can carry the full event, or the title and location only, or nothing but a block reading “Busy” — and individual events can be skipped, hidden, or shared in full with a tag in their notes. If you would rather a provider never hold the details, don’t send them.

The one thing I can't control — and won't pretend to

If your destination calendar lives in an account like iCloud, Google, or Exchange, then once a copy lands there, that provider syncs and retains it under their policies — exactly as it would for any event you added to that calendar yourself. Calendar Mirror isn't part of that sync and can't reach into it: on your device it keeps to itself, and beyond it your account provider's terms apply. If that matters to you, point Calendar Mirror at a local or self-hosted calendar as the destination.

The request page (askwhen.me) — optional, and off until you turn it on

Calendar Mirror 2.0 can give you a page at askwhen.me/yourpage where a stranger can ask you for a time. That page needs a server, and this section is the whole of what that server does with data. The design is called a dead drop: your device works out which times to offer and sends only those; the server holds requests until your device collects them. At no point does the server have your calendar, your email address, or a way to contact you.

What your device sends about you

  • A list of times you are willing to be asked for — start and end, nothing else. Not your busy times, not what fills the gaps between them. A gap on the page might be a meeting or might be a rule you set, and the server cannot tell which.
  • The display name you chose for the page, and an optional line of text. This is the only identifying thing in the document, and you wrote it knowing it is public.
  • A meeting title and length, and your time zone.
  • An anonymous subscription identifier from Apple — a number, which Apple does not tie to your name or email in anything they give me. It is stored hashed. There is no account, because there is nothing to put in one.

Your device replaces that list every few hours. If it stops, the page goes dark within a day rather than serving stale times. Delete the page in the app and everything above is deleted at once, along with every request on it.

What a requester gives, and how long it stays

Someone asking for a time gives their name, email address, and an optional note, and picks a slot. The server holds those only as long as it takes to get an answer to them:

  • Until they confirm their email — one hour. Nothing reaches you until they click the link. If they never do, the request is deleted.
  • Until you answer — up to 14 days. Then they are told nobody answered, and it is deleted.
  • After you answer — until the email reaches them, 48 hours at most. An accepted request needs their address once more, to send them the calendar file; the moment my mail server reports it delivered, the request is deleted. A declined one is deleted the same way after the reply is sent.

These limits are enforced by the database itself, not by a schedule somebody could forget to run. There is no requester account, no history, and nothing that links one request to another.

What the request page does not do

The page loads nothing from anyone else: no fonts, no analytics, no scripts from a third party, no captcha service. It makes exactly two requests, both to askwhen.me. That is checked by the build, which fails if a third appears. Rate limiting exists to stop abuse, but it does not store IP addresses: it stores a keyed hash that rotates daily, after which yesterday's entries cannot be linked to an address even by me.

Email

The confirmation, the answer, and the calendar file are sent from no-reply@askwhen.me through a mail server I run myself, on infrastructure I own. Delivery reports come back from it so that "delivered" can mean the request is deleted; nothing about what an email says is logged. The person who asked gets your display name and the meeting time. They never get your email address — the server does not have it to give.

Custom domains

If you point your own domain at your page, the server stores that hostname and checks, from time to time, that it still points here. That is all it holds about the domain.

Payment

The request page is a subscription bought through the App Store. Apple handles payment; I never see a card, a name, or a billing address. Apple tells my server when a subscription starts, renews, or ends, using the same anonymous identifier as above, so a lapsed page can be taken down.

Where it runs

On a server I operate in Colorado, United States, behind a reverse proxy that keeps ordinary access logs.

Changes

If this policy ever changes, the update will be here and in the source, which is public. The bar it has to clear is simple: the app still collects nothing, and the request page still holds nothing longer than it takes to get someone an answer.
