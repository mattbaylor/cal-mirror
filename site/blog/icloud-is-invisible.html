Your scheduling tool can’t see your iCloud calendar

4-minute read

You pay for a scheduling tool. It defends your focus time, or it hands out a booking link, or it merges your calendars into one clean view. And it cannot see the calendar where your actual life is, because your actual life is in iCloud.

This is not the tools being lazy. Apple gives third parties no push notification for calendar changes, so a hosted service has to poll — and polling iCloud is slow and rate-limited enough that most vendors publish a five-to-ten minute interval for it, if they support it at all. Several support Google and Microsoft only, and say so on their pricing page.

The result is an AI scheduler that books over the dentist, a booking page that offers a slot you gave away yesterday, and a unified view that is missing the half you most needed unified.

Give the tool a calendar it reads properly

Mirror the iCloud calendar into a Google or Microsoft calendar on your own Mac, and point the hosted tool at the copy. Your Mac already has both accounts. It follows the iCloud change soon after it happens and writes the copy into the Google calendar, which the hosted service reads on its fast, real-time path — because as far as it is concerned, this is simply a Google calendar with events in it.

The setup

  1. Make sure both accounts are added in the Calendar app on your Mac: your iCloud account and the Google or Microsoft one the scheduler reads.
  2. Create an empty calendar in the Google or Microsoft account — call it Personal (mirrored) — so the copies never mix with events that account owns.
  3. Pair iCloud → that calendar. Choose Busy only unless the tool genuinely needs titles; it usually doesn’t, and this way your dentist stays yours.
  4. In the scheduling tool, add that calendar as a source of conflicts — not as somewhere to book into.

This turns the slow half of the pipeline into the fast half. The comparison pages spell out the effect per tool: Reclaim gets a week it otherwise can’t defend, OneCal and CalendarBridge get iCloud events on their real-time path instead of a poll, and CalendarSync gets an Apple source it has no adapter for at all.

Bridging adds a hop. Calendar Mirror writes the copy on your device; the other service then reads that copy on its own schedule. The chain is only as fast as its slowest link, and it needs a Mac that is switched on. What it removes is the slowest link.

What this doesn’t fix

The copy is one-way. If the scheduling tool books a meeting, it books it into the Google calendar, not into iCloud — so decide which account is the one you actually keep appointments in, and let the other be the mirror. Attendees are never carried across. And if the tool you are paying for supports nothing but Google and Outlook, this makes it usable, not native: you are handing it a faithful shadow of your iCloud week, not iCloud itself.
